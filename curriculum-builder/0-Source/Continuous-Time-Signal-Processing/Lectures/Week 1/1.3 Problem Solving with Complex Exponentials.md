## Lecture Outline (Complex Exponentials Continued: Phasors, Identities, and Adding Sinusoids)

### 1. Review of the Complex Exponential
- General form:
  $$ z(t) = A e^{j(\omega t + \phi)} $$
- This is the **polar form** of a complex exponential.
- Using Euler’s identity:
  $$ e^{j\theta} = \cos(\theta) + j\sin(\theta) $$
- Therefore:
  $$ z(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) + jA\sin(\omega t + \phi) $$
- Real part:
  $$ \Re\{z(t)\} = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) $$
- Imaginary part:
  $$ \Im\{z(t)\} = A\sin(\omega t + \phi) $$

---

### 2. Converting a Real Sinusoid into Complex Form
- A real cosine signal can be written as:
  $$ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) $$
- This is the real part of a complex exponential:
  $$ x(t) = \Re\left\{A e^{j(\omega t + \phi)}\right\} $$
- Factor the exponential:
  $$ A e^{j(\omega t + \phi)} = A e^{j\phi} e^{j\omega t} $$

---

### 3. Phasor Representation
- Define the **phasor** as:
  $$ V = A e^{j\phi} $$
- Then:
  $$ x(t) = \Re\left\{V e^{j\omega t}\right\} $$
- Interpretation:
  - $A$ is the magnitude of the phasor
  - $\phi$ is its angle
  - $\omega$ is the angular speed of rotation

---

### 4. Direction of Rotation and Sign of Frequency
- If $\omega > 0$, the phasor rotates **counterclockwise**
- If $\omega < 0$, the phasor rotates **clockwise**
- So the sign of frequency determines the direction of rotation in the complex plane.

---

### 5. Unit-Circle Identities from Euler’s Formula
- Start with:
  $$ e^{j\theta} = \cos(\theta) + j\sin(\theta) $$
  $$ e^{-j\theta} = \cos(\theta) - j\sin(\theta) $$
- Add them:
  $$ \cos(\theta) = \frac{e^{j\theta} + e^{-j\theta}}{2} $$
- Subtract them:
  $$ \sin(\theta) = \frac{e^{j\theta} - e^{-j\theta}}{2j} $$

---

### 6. Why These Identities Matter
- They let us move between:
  - **trigonometric form**
  - **complex exponential form**
- This makes many signal calculations much easier in the complex domain.

---

### 7. Cosine as a Sum of Two Complex Exponentials
- Substitute $\theta = \omega t + \phi$:
  $$ \cos(\omega t + \phi) = \frac{e^{j(\omega t + \phi)} + e^{-j(\omega t + \phi)}}{2} $$
- Multiplying by $A$:
  $$ A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \frac{A}{2}e^{j(\omega t + \phi)} + \frac{A}{2}e^{-j(\omega t + \phi)} $$
- Interpretation:
  - a real cosine is the sum of
    - one **positive-frequency** complex exponential
    - one **negative-frequency** complex exponential

---

### 8. Positive and Negative Frequency
- Positive-frequency term:
  $$ e^{j\omega t} $$
  rotates counterclockwise.
- Negative-frequency term:
  $$ e^{-j\omega t} $$
  rotates clockwise.
- A real sinusoid is built from both of these components.

---

### 9. Adding Two Same-Frequency Cosines Using Phasors
- Suppose:
  $$ y(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi_A) + B\cos(\omega t + \phi_B) $$
- Convert both terms to complex form:
  $$ y(t) = \Re\left\{A e^{j\phi_A} e^{j\omega t} + B e^{j\phi_B} e^{j\omega t}\right\} $$
- Define phasors:
  $$ V_1 = A e^{j\phi_A}, \qquad V_2 = B e^{j\phi_B} $$
- Then:
  $$ y(t) = \Re\left\{(V_1 + V_2)e^{j\omega t}\right\} $$
- Since both signals have the same frequency $\omega$, their phasors can be added directly.

---

### 10. Resultant Phasor
- Let:
  $$ V = V_1 + V_2 $$
- Then the final signal becomes:
  $$ y(t) = \Re\left\{V e^{j\omega t}\right\} $$
- If:
  $$ V = |V|e^{j\angle V} $$
- Then:
  $$ y(t) = |V|\cos(\omega t + \angle V) $$

---

### 11. Worked Example
- Given:
  $$ y(t) = \sqrt{3}\cos(\omega t) + \cos\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) $$
- Convert to phasor form:
  $$ y(t) = \Re\left\{\left(\sqrt{3}e^{j0} + e^{j\pi/2}\right)e^{j\omega t}\right\} $$
- Since:
  $$ e^{j\pi/2} = j $$
- The phasor sum is:
  $$ \sqrt{3} + j $$

---

### 12. Convert the Resultant Phasor to Polar Form
- Magnitude:
  $$ \left|\sqrt{3} + j\right| = \sqrt{(\sqrt{3})^2 + 1^2} = 2 $$
- Angle:
  $$ \angle(\sqrt{3} + j) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6} $$
- So:
  $$ \sqrt{3} + j = 2e^{j\pi/6} $$

---

### 13. Final Answer for the Example
- Substitute back:
  $$ y(t) = \Re\left\{2e^{j\pi/6}e^{j\omega t}\right\} $$
- Therefore:
  $$ y(t) = 2\cos\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{6}\right) $$

---

### 14. Main Takeaways
- A real cosine can be written as the real part of a complex exponential.
- A cosine can also be written as the sum of positive- and negative-frequency exponentials.
- Phasors make it easy to add sinusoids with the same frequency.
- The workflow is:
  1. Convert from real sinusoids to phasors
  2. Do the algebra in the complex domain
  3. Convert back by taking the real part
