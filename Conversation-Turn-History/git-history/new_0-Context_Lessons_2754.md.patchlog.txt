+<a id="introduction"></a>
+## Introduction
+
+A graph $G$ is **planar** if it can be drawn in the plane such that its edges intersect only at their endpoints (vertices). Such a drawing is called an *embedding* of $G$ in the plane.
+
+For example, all three graphs shown below are planar, and each image represents an embedding.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/0a2025a3d626587328d2cfa80ae6529b](#)
+
+An embedding of a planar graph divides the plane into regions called **faces**.
+
+A **face** is a connected region of the plane bounded by the graph's edges in a given embedding. The **outer face** is the unbounded region that extends infinitely beyond the graph.
+
+For example, consider the graph below.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/74bb796251bc3e0e534315525cd04f91](#)
+
+This graph is planar, and its image represents an embedding. The $3$ faces of the graph, including the outer face, are listed below.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/5555ecd790891fe0f7170bfac041971c](#)
+
+Each face is a region in the plane bounded by a cycle. For example:
+
+- Face I is bounded by the cycle $v_1-v_2-v_3-v_4-v_1$.
+- Face II is bounded by the cycle $v_1-v_2-v_4-v_1$.
+- Face III is bounded by the cycle $v_2-v_3-v_4-v_2$.
+
+For simple graphs, each of these bounding cycles must contain at least three edges. This fact becomes important in a proof that we'll study later.
+

+<a id="eulers-formula-for-planar-graphs"></a>
+## Euler's Formula for Planar Graphs
+
+For any connected planar graph with $v$ vertices, $e$ edges, and $f$ faces, **Euler's formula** for planar graphs states that
+
+$$
+v - e + f = 2
+$$
+
+Recall that an undirected graph is *connected* if there is a path between every pair of vertices. If you've studied geometry, this is closely related to Euler's formula for polyhedra.
+
+For example, consider a planar graph with $8$ vertices and $12$ edges.
+
+Let's find the number of faces, $f$, by substituting $v=8$ and $e=12$ into Euler's formula:
+
+$$
+\begin{aligned}
+v - e + f &= 2 \\
+8 - 12 + f &= 2 \\
+-4 + f &= 2 \\
+f &= 6
+\end{aligned}
+$$
+
+Therefore, the graph has $6$ faces.
+
+We can verify this result by examining the graph below, which has $8$ vertices and $12$ edges. It indeed has $6$ faces.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/caff607ac2437d69900205aa13e850b7](#)

+<a id="using-eulers-formula-for-planar-graphs"></a>
+## Using Euler's Formula for Planar Graphs
+
+**Example:** If a planar graph has $12$ edges and $8$ faces, then how many vertices does it have?
+
+**Explanation**
+
+Euler's formula for planar graphs states that
+
+$$
+v - e + f = 2
+$$
+
+where $v$ is the number of vertices, $e$ is the number of edges, and $f$ is the number of faces.
+
+Substituting $e=12$ and $f=8$ into the formula, we solve for $v$:
+
+$$
+\begin{aligned}
+v - e + f &= 2 \\
+v - 12 + 8 &= 2 \\
+v - 4 &= 2 \\
+v &= 6
+\end{aligned}
+$$
+
+Therefore, the graph has $6$ vertices.
+

+<a id="the-complete-graph-k5-is-not-planar"></a>
+## The Complete Graph K(5) is not Planar
+
+The complete graphs $K_1$, $K_2$, $K_3$, and $K_4$ are planar, as illustrated below.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/f9204acc5955850c0c4766a228467b8b](#)
+
+However, the complete graph $K_5$ is not planar; it cannot be drawn in the plane without edges crossing.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/b567c420824bbcf1b9dc96a6bf4e6caf](#)
+
+To prove that $K_5$ is not planar, we use proof by contradiction.
+
+Assume, for contradiction, that $K_5$ is planar. Then it must satisfy Euler's formula:
+
+$$
+v - e + f = 2
+$$
+
+The number of edges in a complete graph $K_n$ is
+
+$$
+e = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}
+$$
+
+Since $K_5$ has $5$ vertices, it has
+
+$$
+e = \frac{5(5-1)}{2} = 10
+$$
+
+Substituting $v=5$ and $e=10$ into Euler's formula gives
+
+$$
+5 - 10 + f = 2
+$$
+
+so
+
+$$
+f = 7
+$$
+
+Because $K_5$ is a simple graph, each face boundary must contain at least $3$ edges. Thus, the graph must have at least
+
+$$
+3f = 3(7) = 21
+$$
+
+face-edge incidences.
+
+But each edge in a planar graph can be incident to at most two faces, so there can be at most
+
+$$
+2e = 2(10) = 20
+$$
+
+face-edge incidences.
+
+This gives the contradiction
+
+$$
+21 \le 20
+$$
+
+which is false.
+
+Therefore, $K_5$ is **not** planar.
+

+<a id="the-complete-bipartite-graph-k33-is-not-planar"></a>
+## The Complete Bipartite Graph K(3,3) is not Planar
+
+The complete bipartite graph $K_{3,3}$ is not planar because it cannot be drawn in the plane without edges crossing.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/dc34e8020568f3d626322539d2b84401](#)
+
+To prove that $K_{3,3}$ is not planar, we use proof by contradiction.
+
+Assume, for contradiction, that $K_{3,3}$ is planar. Then it must satisfy Euler's formula:
+
+$$
+v - e + f = 2
+$$
+
+For a complete bipartite graph $K_{m,n}$,
+
+$$
+v = m+n
+\qquad \text{and} \qquad
+e = mn
+$$
+
+Hence, $K_{3,3}$ has
+
+$$
+v = 3+3 = 6
+\qquad \text{and} \qquad
+e = 3 \cdot 3 = 9
+$$
+
+Substituting $v=6$ and $e=9$ into Euler's formula gives
+
+$$
+6 - 9 + f = 2
+$$
+
+so
+
+$$
+f = 5
+$$
+
+Since a bipartite graph cannot contain odd cycles, each face boundary must contain at least $4$ edges. Thus, the graph must have at least
+
+$$
+4f = 4(5) = 20
+$$
+
+face-edge incidences.
+
+But each edge can be incident to at most two faces, so there can be at most
+
+$$
+2e = 2(9) = 18
+$$
+
+face-edge incidences.
+
+This gives the contradiction
+
+$$
+20 \le 18
+$$
+
+which is false.
+
+Therefore, $K_{3,3}$ is not planar.
+

+<a id="proving-a-graph-is-not-planar"></a>
+## Proving a Graph is Not Planar
+
+**Example:** Prove the following statement:
+
+*The graph $K_6$, the complete graph with $6$ vertices, is not planar.*
+
+**Explanation**
+
+We use proof by contradiction. Assume that $K_6$ is planar. Then it must satisfy Euler's formula:
+
+$$
+v - e + f = 2
+$$
+
+The number of edges in a complete graph $K_n$ is
+
+$$
+e = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}
+$$
+
+Since $K_6$ has $6$ vertices, it has
+
+$$
+e = \frac{6(6-1)}{2} = 15
+$$
+
+Substituting $v=6$ and $e=15$ into Euler's formula gives
+
+$$
+6 - 15 + f = 2
+$$
+
+so
+
+$$
+f = 11
+$$
+
+Because each face boundary must contain at least $3$ edges, the graph must have at least
+
+$$
+3f = 3(11) = 33
+$$
+
+face-edge incidences.
+
+But each edge can be incident to at most two faces, so there can be at most
+
+$$
+2e = 2(15) = 30
+$$
+
+face-edge incidences.
+
+This gives the contradiction
+
+$$
+33 \le 30
+$$
+
+which is false.
+
+Therefore, $K_6$ is not planar.
+

+<a id="elementary-subdivisions"></a>
+## Elementary Subdivisions
+
+An **elementary subdivision** of a loop-free graph occurs when an edge $u-v$ is removed, and a new vertex $w$ is introduced, with edges $u-w$ and $w-v$ added to the graph. In effect, this splits the original edge into two by inserting a new vertex.
+
+If $G'$ is an elementary subdivision of a planar graph $G$, then $G'$ is also planar. We can obtain a planar embedding of $G'$ by placing the new vertex in the middle of the edge being subdivided.
+
+For example, let's apply an elementary subdivision to the edge $v_2-v_3$ in the graph below.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/fd5345d1eb2321da9b1ca86d2a06f539](#)
+
+1. First, remove the edge $v_2-v_3$.
+
+   [Image Placeholder: /graphics/dfa0d10e3340e1b1845bd5600784784b](#)
+
+2. Next, add a new vertex $v_4$.
+
+   [Image Placeholder: /graphics/ec5ecbcd0c24523b87cf843ee23c4d0e](#)
+
+3. Finally, connect the new vertex to the two original vertices by adding the edges $v_2-v_4$ and $v_4-v_3$.
+
+   [Image Placeholder: /graphics/c21e28af9f28d5e8629109c6ac4d45ec](#)
+
+Note that the original graph was planar, and so is the new graph.
+

+## Homeomorphic Graphs
+
+Two loop-free graphs are **homeomorphic** if they are isomorphic, or if both can be obtained from the same loop-free undirected graph through a sequence of elementary subdivisions.
+
+For example, consider the two graphs below.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/84c2d1a9915d36abbc7ea25ab85dd27e](#)
+
+1. First, remove the edge $v_2-v_3$.
+
+   [Image Placeholder: /graphics/bb81cda874f00af071984f091694ce1f](#)
+
+2. Next, add a new vertex $v_4$.
+
+   [Image Placeholder: /graphics/6faec3c981e6b37eb025918ff843dce1](#)
+
+3. Finally, connect the new vertex to the two original vertices by adding the edges $v_2-v_4$ and $v_4-v_3$.
+
+   [Image Placeholder: /graphics/c19065d6ab47b3a59cf7d27e894225e8](#)
+
+Therefore, since it is possible to obtain the graph $H$ by subdividing the edge $v_2-v_3$ in the graph $G$, the graphs $G$ and $H$ are homeomorphic.

+<a id="introduction"></a>
+## Introduction
+
+A graph $G$ is **planar** if it can be drawn in the plane such that its edges intersect only at their endpoints (vertices). Such a drawing is called an *embedding* of $G$ in the plane.
+
+For example, all three graphs shown below are planar, and each image represents an embedding.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/0a2025a3d626587328d2cfa80ae6529b](#)
+
+An embedding of a planar graph divides the plane into regions called **faces**.
+
+A **face** is a connected region of the plane bounded by the graph's edges in a given embedding. The **outer face** is the unbounded region that extends infinitely beyond the graph.
+
+For example, consider the graph below.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/74bb796251bc3e0e534315525cd04f91](#)
+
+This graph is planar, and its image represents an embedding. The $3$ faces of the graph, including the outer face, are listed below.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/5555ecd790891fe0f7170bfac041971c](#)
+
+Each face is a region in the plane bounded by a cycle. For example:
+
+- Face I is bounded by the cycle $v_1-v_2-v_3-v_4-v_1$.
+- Face II is bounded by the cycle $v_1-v_2-v_4-v_1$.
+- Face III is bounded by the cycle $v_2-v_3-v_4-v_2$.
+
+For simple graphs, each of these bounding cycles must contain at least three edges. This fact becomes important in a proof that we'll study later.
+

+<a id="eulers-formula-for-planar-graphs"></a>
+## Euler's Formula for Planar Graphs
+
+For any connected planar graph with $v$ vertices, $e$ edges, and $f$ faces, **Euler's formula** for planar graphs states that
+
+$$
+v - e + f = 2
+$$
+
+Recall that an undirected graph is *connected* if there is a path between every pair of vertices. If you've studied geometry, this is closely related to Euler's formula for polyhedra.
+
+For example, consider a planar graph with $8$ vertices and $12$ edges.
+
+Let's find the number of faces, $f$, by substituting $v=8$ and $e=12$ into Euler's formula:
+
+$$
+\begin{aligned}
+v - e + f &= 2 \\
+8 - 12 + f &= 2 \\
+-4 + f &= 2 \\
+f &= 6
+\end{aligned}
+$$
+
+Therefore, the graph has $6$ faces.
+
+We can verify this result by examining the graph below, which has $8$ vertices and $12$ edges. It indeed has $6$ faces.
+
+[Image Placeholder: /graphics/caff607ac2437d69900205aa13e850b7](#)

+<a id="using-eulers-formula-for-planar-graphs"></a>
+## Using Euler's Formula for Planar Graphs
+
+**Example:** If a planar graph has $12$ edges and $8$ faces, then how many vertices does it have?
+
+**Explanation**
+
+Euler's formula for planar graphs states that
+
+$$
+v - e + f = 2
+$$
+
+where $v$ is the number of vertices, $e$ is the number of edges, and $f$ is the number of faces.
+
+Substituting $e=12$ and $f=8$ into the formula, we solve for $v$:
+
+$$
+\begin{aligned}
+v - e + f &= 2 \\
+v - 12 + 8 &= 2 \\
+v - 4 &= 2 \\
+v &= 6
+\end{aligned}
+$$
+
+Therefore, the graph has $6$ vertices.
