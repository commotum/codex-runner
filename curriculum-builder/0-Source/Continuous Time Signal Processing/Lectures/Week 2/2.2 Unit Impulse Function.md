## Lecture Outline (Unit Impulse Function and Its Key Properties)

### 1. Unit Impulse Function
- The **unit impulse function** is denoted by:
  $$ \delta(t) $$
- It is zero everywhere except at $t=0$:
  $$ \delta(t) = 0, \quad t \ne 0 $$
- Its defining property is:
  $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t)\,dt = 1 $$
- Graphically, it is drawn as an impulse at $t=0$ with **unit area**.

---

### 2. Important Interpretation
- In continuous time, the impulse does **not** have amplitude $1$ in the ordinary sense.
- Instead, it has:
  - zero width
  - infinitely large height
  - finite area equal to $1$
- So the “$1$” attached to the impulse symbol represents its **weight / area**, not its amplitude.

---

### 3. Comparison with the Discrete-Time Impulse
- In discrete time:
  $$
  \delta[n] =
  \begin{cases}
  1, & n=0 \\
  0, & n\ne 0
  \end{cases}
  $$
- In continuous time, $\delta(t)$ is different:
  - it is not an ordinary function value of $1$ at $t=0$
  - it is a generalized function with unit area

---

### 4. Approximating the Impulse with a Narrow Pulse
- A useful approximation is a rectangular pulse:
  $$
  \delta_\varepsilon(t)=
  \begin{cases}
  \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}, & -\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \le t \le \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \\
  0, & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  $$
- Its width is:
  $$ \varepsilon $$
- Its height is:
  $$ \frac{1}{\varepsilon} $$
- Its area is:
  $$ \varepsilon \cdot \frac{1}{\varepsilon} = 1 $$
- As $\varepsilon \to 0$:
  - width $\to 0$
  - height $\to \infty$
  - area stays $1$
- This limiting idea leads to the continuous-time impulse $\delta(t)$.

---

### 5. Generalized Function Viewpoint
- The impulse is not an ordinary function in the usual sense.
- It is better understood by **how it acts on other functions**, especially under integration.
- This is why $\delta(t)$ is often called a **generalized function**.

---

### 6. Scaled Impulse
- If the impulse is scaled by a constant $k$:
  $$ k\delta(t) $$
- Then its total area becomes:
  $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} k\delta(t)\,dt = k $$
- So $k$ represents the **weight / area** of the impulse.

---

### 7. Multiplying a Function by an Impulse
- Let $f(t)$ be continuous at $t=0$.
- Then:
  $$ f(t)\delta(t) = f(0)\delta(t) $$
- Intuition:
  - the impulse only “sees” the value of the function at the location where the impulse is placed
  - everything else is suppressed

---

### 8. Shifted Impulse
- A shifted impulse is written as:
  $$ \delta(t-T) $$
- This moves the impulse from $t=0$ to $t=T$.
- Its area is still:
  $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t-T)\,dt = 1 $$

---

### 9. Multiplication with a Shifted Impulse
- If $f(t)$ is continuous at $t=T$, then:
  $$ f(t)\delta(t-T) = f(T)\delta(t-T) $$
- The impulse keeps only the value of the function at the shifted location $t=T$.

---

### 10. Sampling / Sifting Property
- Integrating the product with a shifted impulse gives:
  $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\delta(t-T)\,dt = f(T) $$
- This is called the:
  - **sampling property**
  - or **sifting property**
- Meaning:
  - the impulse extracts the value of the function at a specific time

---

### 11. Why the Sampling Property Matters
- If you want the value of a function at $t=T$, you can use:
  $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\delta(t-T)\,dt $$
- The result is:
  $$ f(T) $$
- This makes the impulse extremely powerful in signals and systems.

---

### 12. Relationship Between Unit Impulse and Unit Step
- The unit step function can be obtained by integrating the impulse:
  $$ u(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} \delta(\tau)\,d\tau $$
- Using the lecture’s convention, this gives:
  $$
  u(t)=
  \begin{cases}
  0, & t<0 \\
  1, & t\ge 0
  \end{cases}
  $$

---

### 13. Derivative Relationship
- Since integration and differentiation are inverse operations:
  $$ \delta(t) = \frac{d}{dt}u(t) $$
- So:
  - integrating the impulse gives the step
  - differentiating the step gives the impulse

---

### 14. Main Takeaways
- The unit impulse is defined by:
  $$ \delta(t)=0 \text{ for } t\ne 0, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t)\,dt=1 $$
- It is a generalized function with **unit area**.
- Scaling gives:
  $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} k\delta(t)\,dt = k $$
- Multiplication with an impulse localizes a function:
  $$ f(t)\delta(t-T)=f(T)\delta(t-T) $$
- Integration gives the sampling property:
  $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\delta(t-T)\,dt = f(T) $$
- The step and impulse are related by:
  $$ u(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\delta(\tau)\,d\tau $$
  $$ \delta(t)=\frac{d}{dt}u(t) $$
