## Lecture Outline (From Fourier Series to Fourier Transform)

### 1. Review of Fourier Series for Periodic Signals
- For a periodic signal $x(t)$, the Fourier series representation is:
  $$
  x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} A_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}
  $$
- Interpretation:
  - $e^{jk\omega_0 t}$ represents complex exponentials, which are equivalent to sinusoids
  - $A_k$ is a complex coefficient that captures:
    - magnitude
    - phase
- So $A_k$ tells us:
  - how much of each sinusoid is present
  - how much phase shift it has

---

### 2. Fourier Series as a Basis Expansion
- Fourier series decomposes a **periodic** signal into a sum of sinusoidal basis functions.
- The frequencies are discrete:
  $$
  k\omega_0, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z}
  $$
- This means the spectrum of a periodic signal consists of discrete frequency components.

---

### 3. Discrete Spectrum for Periodic Signals
- In Fourier series, the signal is built from harmonics of the fundamental frequency:
  $$
  \omega_0, \; 2\omega_0, \; 3\omega_0, \dots
  $$
- So the spectrum is discrete in frequency.
- The coefficient plot $A_k$ versus $k$ shows:
  - which harmonics are present
  - how strongly each harmonic contributes to forming $x(t)$

---

### 4. Motivation for Fourier Transform
- Real-world signals are often **aperiodic**, not periodic.
- Example:
  - a single pulse that does not repeat
- The question is:
  - how can we generalize Fourier series to work for nonperiodic signals?

---

### 5. Aperiodic Signal as a Limiting Case of a Periodic Signal
- An aperiodic signal can be viewed as a periodic signal whose period becomes very large.
- If we repeat an aperiodic pulse with larger and larger period $T$, the copies move farther apart.
- In the limit:
  $$
  T \to \infty
  $$
- The repeated copies become infinitely far away, so only the original pulse remains visible.
- This gives the intuition that an aperiodic signal can be treated as the limit of a periodic signal as:
  $$
  T \to \infty
  $$

---

### 6. What Happens to Frequency Spacing?
- Since:
  $$
  f = \frac{1}{T}
  $$
- If:
  $$
  T \to \infty
  $$
  then:
  $$
  f \to 0
  $$
- Likewise, the fundamental angular frequency spacing goes to zero.
- This means the discrete harmonic frequencies of Fourier series become **continuous**.

---

### 7. Transition from Fourier Series to Fourier Transform
- In Fourier series:
  - frequency values are discrete
  - the representation uses a summation
- In Fourier transform:
  - frequency becomes continuous
  - the representation uses an integral
- So the sum over discrete frequencies becomes an integral over continuous frequency.

---

### 8. Fourier Transform Definition
- For a general aperiodic signal, the Fourier transform is defined as:
  $$
  X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
  $$
- This is the **forward Fourier transform** or **analysis equation**.

---

### 9. Inverse Fourier Transform
- The original time-domain signal can be reconstructed from its spectrum using:
  $$
  x(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(j\omega)e^{j\omega t}\,d\omega
  $$
- This is the **inverse Fourier transform**.

---

### 10. Time Domain and Frequency Domain
- Fourier transform lets us move between:
  - time domain:
    $$ x(t) $$
  - frequency domain:
    $$ X(j\omega) $$
- Symbolically:
  $$
  x(t) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; X(j\omega)
  $$
- The Fourier transform gives the frequency content of the signal.

---

### 11. Key Difference: Fourier Series vs. Fourier Transform
- **Fourier series**:
  - applies to periodic signals
  - uses discrete frequencies
  - uses a sum
- **Fourier transform**:
  - applies to general aperiodic signals
  - uses continuous frequency
  - uses an integral

---

### 12. Example: Fourier Transform of a One-Sided Exponential
- Given:
  $$
  x(t) = e^{-7t}u(t)
  $$
- The unit step $u(t)$ makes the signal one-sided:
  - zero for $t<0$
  - exponential decay for $t\ge 0$

---

### 13. Set Up the Transform Integral
- Since $x(t)=0$ for $t<0$, the Fourier transform becomes:
  $$
  X(j\omega) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-7t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
  $$
- Combine the exponentials:
  $$
  X(j\omega) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-(7+j\omega)t}\,dt
  $$

---

### 14. Evaluate the Integral
- Integrating:
  $$
  X(j\omega) = \left[\frac{e^{-(7+j\omega)t}}{-(7+j\omega)}\right]_0^{\infty}
  $$
- Result:
  $$
  X(j\omega) = \frac{1}{7+j\omega}
  $$

---

### 15. Transform Pair from the Example
- The lecture establishes the transform pair:
  $$
  e^{-7t}u(t) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{7+j\omega}
  $$
- This means:
  - time-domain exponential decay corresponds to a rational function in frequency

---

### 16. Practical Note About the Inverse Transform
- In principle, we can recover $x(t)$ by applying the inverse Fourier transform:
  $$
  x(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{7+j\omega} e^{j\omega t}\,d\omega
  $$
- But in practice, once a transform pair is known, we usually use the pair directly rather than computing the inverse integral from scratch.

---

### 17. Main Takeaways
- Fourier series represents periodic signals using discrete harmonics:
  $$
  x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} A_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}
  $$
- Fourier transform generalizes this idea to aperiodic signals by letting the period go to infinity.
- As:
  $$
  T \to \infty
  $$
  the frequency spacing goes to zero, so the spectrum becomes continuous.
- The Fourier transform pair is:
  $$
  X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
  $$
  $$
  x(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(j\omega)e^{j\omega t}\,d\omega
  $$
- Example:
  $$
  e^{-7t}u(t) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{7+j\omega}
  $$
