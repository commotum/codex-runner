## Lecture Outline (Fourier Series Representation of Periodic Signals)

### 1. Periodic Signal Review
- A periodic signal satisfies:
  $$ x(t) = x(t+T) $$
- Here, $T$ is the period of the signal.
- The lecture begins with an arbitrary periodic waveform $x(t)$ and asks how it can be represented using simpler building blocks.

---

### 2. Fourier’s Main Idea
- A periodic signal can be represented as a sum of sinusoids.
- These sinusoids are chosen so that, when added together, they reconstruct the original periodic signal.
- The lecture emphasizes that this is the key idea discovered by Fourier:
  - complicated periodic signals can be decomposed into simpler sinusoidal components.

---

### 3. Example Decomposition into Components
- The lecture illustrates a periodic signal as a sum of:
  - a DC component
  - cosine components with different amplitudes
  - higher-frequency harmonic terms
- Example components shown include:
  $$ x_0(t) = 1 $$
  $$ x_1(t) = \frac{1}{2}\cos(2\pi t) $$
  $$ x_2(t) = \cos(4\pi t) $$
  $$ x_3(t) = \frac{2}{3}\cos(6\pi t) $$
- Adding these components reconstructs the original periodic signal:
  $$ x(t) = x_0(t) + x_1(t) + x_2(t) + x_3(t) $$

---

### 4. DC Component
- The constant term:
  $$ x_0(t) = 1 $$
  is called the **DC component**.
- This accounts for any vertical shift of the periodic signal.
- In the lecture example, the signal appears shifted upward, so a nonzero DC term is required.

---

### 5. Fundamental Frequency and Harmonics
- The first sinusoidal term uses the **fundamental frequency**:
  $$ \omega_0 = 2\pi $$
- Higher-frequency terms are integer multiples of the fundamental:
  $$ 2\omega_0,\; 3\omega_0,\; \dots $$
- These are called **harmonic frequencies**.
- Example:
  $$ 4\pi = 2\omega_0 $$
  $$ 6\pi = 3\omega_0 $$

---

### 6. Fourier’s Statement
- The lecture states that any periodic signal can be decomposed into a sum of sinusoids with harmonic frequencies.
- In words:
  - start with the fundamental
  - add harmonics
  - choose the correct coefficients
  - reconstruct the signal

---

### 7. Fourier Series Form
- The periodic signal is written as:
  $$
  x(t) = b_0 + b_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + b_2\cos(2\omega_0 t) + b_3\cos(3\omega_0 t) + \cdots
  $$
  $$
  \quad\quad + a_1\sin(\omega_0 t) + a_2\sin(2\omega_0 t) + a_3\sin(3\omega_0 t) + \cdots
  $$
- Here:
  - $b_0$ is the DC term
  - $b_k$ are cosine coefficients
  - $a_k$ are sine coefficients

---

### 8. Compact Summation Form
- The same series can be written more compactly as:
  $$
  x(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left[ b_k \cos(k\omega_0 t) + a_k \sin(k\omega_0 t) \right]
  $$
- Notes:
  - when $k=0$, the cosine term becomes:
    $$ \cos(0)=1 $$
    so this gives the DC term $b_0$
  - the sine term at $k=0$ is zero:
    $$ \sin(0)=0 $$
    so there is no $a_0$ term

---

### 9. Meaning of the Fourier Coefficients
- The coefficients $a_k$ and $b_k$ tell us how much of each sinusoid must be added.
- They are the **weights** of the basis sinusoids.
- In the lecture example:
  $$ b_0 = 1 $$
  $$ b_1 = \frac{1}{2} $$
  $$ b_2 = 1 $$
  $$ b_3 = \frac{2}{3} $$
- For that example:
  $$ a_1 = a_2 = a_3 = \cdots = 0 $$
- So only cosine terms are needed for that particular waveform.

---

### 10. Frequency-Domain Interpretation
- Instead of viewing the signal in time, we can represent it in terms of its frequency components.
- The lecture shows that each harmonic has:
  - a frequency location
  - an associated coefficient magnitude
- For the example:
  - at
    $$ \omega = 2\pi $$
    the coefficient is
    $$ \frac{1}{2} $$
  - at
    $$ \omega = 4\pi $$
    the coefficient is
    $$ 1 $$
  - at
    $$ \omega = 6\pi $$
    the coefficient is
    $$ \frac{2}{3} $$

---

### 11. Harmonic Index Representation
- The same information can also be represented using the harmonic index $k$ instead of frequency $\omega$.
- For the example:
  - $k=1$ corresponds to the fundamental
  - $k=2$ corresponds to the second harmonic
  - $k=3$ corresponds to the third harmonic
- Their coefficients are:
  $$ b_1 = \frac{1}{2}, \quad b_2 = 1, \quad b_3 = \frac{2}{3} $$

---

### 12. Time-Domain vs. Frequency-Domain Views
- **Time-domain view**:
  - add sines and cosines to reconstruct $x(t)$
- **Frequency-domain view**:
  - look at the coefficient values associated with each harmonic
- These are two different ways to describe the same signal.

---

### 13. Sine and Cosine as Basis Functions
- The lecture describes sine and cosine functions as **basis functions**.
- Just as vectors can be written in terms of basis vectors, periodic signals can be written in terms of sinusoidal basis functions.
- The idea is:
  - the signal $x(t)$ is analogous to a vector
  - the sine and cosine terms act like basis directions
  - the coefficients $a_k$ and $b_k$ act like the coordinates in that basis

---

### 14. Basis-Vector Analogy
- In 2D vector form:
  $$ \mathbf{A} = A_x \hat{i} + A_y \hat{j} $$
- The lecture compares this to Fourier series:
  - $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ are like basis vectors
  - sine and cosine functions are like basis functions
  - $A_x$ and $A_y$ are like Fourier coefficients
- Difference:
  - vector spaces here use finitely many basis vectors
  - Fourier series uses infinitely many sinusoidal basis functions

---

### 15. Main Takeaways
- A periodic signal can be reconstructed from sinusoids.
- The frequencies used are the fundamental and its harmonics:
  $$ k\omega_0, \quad k=1,2,3,\dots $$
- The Fourier coefficients determine the contribution of each sinusoid.
- The Fourier series can be written as:
  $$
  x(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left[ b_k \cos(k\omega_0 t) + a_k \sin(k\omega_0 t) \right]
  $$
- Sine and cosine act as basis functions for representing periodic signals.
