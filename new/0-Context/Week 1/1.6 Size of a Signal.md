## Lecture Outline (Signal Size, Energy, and Power)

### 1. Signal Size
- The **size of a signal** is a single number that indicates the strength of that signal.
- Since a signal’s amplitude can vary with time, signal size should account for:
  - amplitude
  - duration

---

### 2. Instantaneous Power from a Circuit Example
- Consider a voltage source $v(t)$ driving current $i(t)$ through a resistor $R$.
- Instantaneous power:
  $$ p(t) = v(t)i(t) $$
- Using Ohm’s law:
  $$ v(t) = i(t)R $$
- So power can also be written as:
  $$ p(t) = \frac{v^2(t)}{R} $$
- For the lecture, assume:
  $$ R = 1 \ \omega $$
- Then:
  $$ p(t) = v^2(t) $$

---

### 3. Relationship Between Power and Energy
- Power is the time derivative of energy:
  $$ p(t) = \frac{dw}{dt} $$
- Therefore, energy is obtained by integration:
  $$ w = \int p(t)\,dt $$

---

### 4. Signal Energy
- Generalizing from voltage to any signal $x(t)$, define the **signal energy** as:
  $$ E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2\,dt $$
- This produces a **single scalar number** describing the total energy in the signal.

---

### 5. When Energy Becomes Infinite
- If $x(t)$ does **not** approach zero as $t \to \infty$, then:
  $$ E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2\,dt $$
  may diverge.
- In that case, energy is not the most useful measure of signal size.

---

### 6. Signal Power
- For signals whose energy is infinite, define the **average power**:
  $$ P = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} |x(t)|^2\,dt $$
- This is also a **single scalar number** representing signal strength.

---

### 7. Power of a Periodic Signal
- If $x(t)$ is periodic with fundamental period $T_0$, then its average power can be computed over one period:
  $$ P = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T_0} |x(t)|^2\,dt $$
- This works because the signal repeats every period.

---

### 8. Example 1: Finite-Energy Signal
- Signal description:
  - constant value $2$ over $-1 \le t \le 0$
  - exponentially decaying for $t \ge 0$
- Interpreted as:
  $$
  x(t)=
  \begin{cases}
  2, & -1 \le t \le 0 \\
  2e^{-t/2}, & t \ge 0 \\
  0, & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  $$
- Energy calculation:
  $$ E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2\,dt $$
- Split into two parts:
  $$ E = \int_{-1}^{0} 2^2\,dt + \int_{0}^{\infty} \left(2e^{-t/2}\right)^2\,dt $$
- Simplify:
  $$ E = \int_{-1}^{0} 4\,dt + \int_{0}^{\infty} 4e^{-t}\,dt $$
- Result:
  $$ E = 4 + 4 = 8 $$

---

### 9. Example 2: Periodic Signal
- The second example is a periodic ramp / sawtooth-type signal.
- Over one fundamental period $[-1,1]$, the signal is:
  $$ x(t) = t $$
- Since the signal is periodic, use average power instead of total energy:
  $$ P = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} x^2(t)\,dt $$
- Substitute $x(t)=t$:
  $$ P = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} t^2\,dt $$
- Result:
  $$ P = \frac{1}{3} $$

---

### 10. Energy Signals vs. Power Signals
- A signal is typically treated as an **energy signal** if:
  $$ 0 < E < \infty $$
- A signal is typically treated as a **power signal** if:
  $$ 0 < P < \infty $$
- Decaying signals are often energy signals.
- Periodic signals are often power signals.

---

### 11. Main Takeaways
- Signal size is described by a single number.
- **Energy** is defined by:
  $$ E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2\,dt $$
- **Average power** is defined by:
  $$ P = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}|x(t)|^2\,dt $$
- For periodic signals:
  $$ P = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T_0}|x(t)|^2\,dt $$
- Example results from the lecture:
  $$ E = 8 $$
  $$ P = \frac{1}{3} $$
