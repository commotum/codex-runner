## Lecture Outline (Convolution and the Convolution Theorem for Fourier Transforms)

### 1. Definition of Convolution
- For two functions $f(t)$ and $g(t)$, the convolution is defined by:
  $$ (f * g)(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(\tau)\,g(t-\tau)\,d\tau $$
- The symbol $*$ means **convolution**, not ordinary multiplication.

---

### 2. Geometric / Overlap Interpretation of Convolution
- To visualize convolution:
  1. Start with $f(\tau)$ and $g(\tau)$
  2. Reflect $g(\tau)$ to get:
     $$ g(-\tau) $$
  3. Shift it by $t$ to get:
     $$ g(t-\tau) $$
  4. Multiply:
     $$ f(\tau)g(t-\tau) $$
  5. Integrate over all $\tau$

- The convolution value at time $t$ depends on **how much the two functions overlap**.

---

### 3. Behavior of the Convolution During the Sweep
- If the nonzero parts of $f$ and $g(t-\tau)$ do **not overlap**, then:
  $$ (f*g)(t)=0 $$
- As overlap begins, the convolution becomes positive or nonzero.
- As the overlap increases, the convolution increases.
- When the overlap is maximum, the convolution reaches its peak.
- As the overlap decreases, the convolution decreases back toward zero.

---

### 4. Example Intuition: Two Box Functions
- If both $f$ and $g$ are rectangular pulse / box functions:
  - the convolution starts at zero
  - rises as the boxes begin to overlap
  - peaks when they overlap most
  - then falls back to zero as they separate
- So the convolution of two box functions has a **triangular shape**.

---

### 5. Big Idea Behind Convolution
- Convolution measures the degree of overlap between one function and a shifted, reflected version of another.
- The process runs over the full range:
  $$ t \in (-\infty,\infty) $$

---

### 6. Fourier Transform Convention Used in the Lecture
- The Fourier transform of a function $h(t)$ is defined as:
  $$ \mathcal{F}\{h(t)\} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi i\omega t}\,h(t)\,dt $$
- In particular:
  $$ F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi i\omega t}f(t)\,dt $$
  $$ G(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi i\omega t}g(t)\,dt $$

---

### 7. Convolution Theorem for Fourier Transforms
- The convolution theorem states:
  $$ \mathcal{F}\{f*g\} = F(\omega)G(\omega) $$
- In words:
  - the Fourier transform of a convolution is the product of the individual Fourier transforms

---

### 8. Start of the Proof
- Begin with the Fourier transform of the convolution:
  $$ \mathcal{F}\{f*g\}
     =
     \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
     e^{-2\pi i\omega t}
     \left(
       \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
       f(\tau)\,g(t-\tau)\,d\tau
     \right)dt $$
- This is a double integral.

---

### 9. Switch the Order of Integration
- Assuming the functions are sufficiently nice, interchange the integrals:
  $$ \mathcal{F}\{f*g\}
     =
     \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
     \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
     e^{-2\pi i\omega t}f(\tau)g(t-\tau)\,dt\,d\tau $$
- Since $f(\tau)$ does not depend on $t$, pull it outside the inner integral:
  $$ \mathcal{F}\{f*g\}
     =
     \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
     f(\tau)
     \left(
       \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
       e^{-2\pi i\omega t}g(t-\tau)\,dt
     \right)d\tau $$

---

### 10. Change of Variables
- Let:
  $$ u=t-\tau $$
- Then:
  $$ t=\tau+u, \qquad du=dt $$
- Substitute into the inner integral:
  $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi i\omega(\tau+u)}g(u)\,du $$
- Split the exponential:
  $$ e^{-2\pi i\omega(\tau+u)} = e^{-2\pi i\omega\tau}e^{-2\pi i\omega u} $$

---

### 11. Separate the Product
- This gives:
  $$ \mathcal{F}\{f*g\}
     =
     \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
     f(\tau)e^{-2\pi i\omega\tau}
     \left(
       \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
       e^{-2\pi i\omega u}g(u)\,du
     \right)d\tau $$
- The inner integral is independent of $\tau$, so it factors out:
  $$ \mathcal{F}\{f*g\}
     =
     \left(
       \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
       e^{-2\pi i\omega u}g(u)\,du
     \right)
     \left(
       \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
       e^{-2\pi i\omega\tau}f(\tau)\,d\tau
     \right) $$

---

### 12. Recognize the Fourier Transforms
- By definition:
  $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi i\omega u}g(u)\,du = G(\omega) $$
  $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2\pi i\omega\tau}f(\tau)\,d\tau = F(\omega) $$
- Therefore:
  $$ \mathcal{F}\{f*g\} = F(\omega)G(\omega) $$

---

### 13. Why the Convolution Theorem Matters
- Convolution in the time domain becomes multiplication in the frequency domain.
- This is very useful when solving differential equations with Fourier transforms.
- It turns a difficult integral operation into a simpler algebraic product.

---

### 14. Connection to Laplace Transforms
- Laplace transforms also have a convolution theorem.
- The main difference is the domain:
  - Laplace transforms typically use:
    $$ t \in [0,\infty) $$
  - Fourier transforms typically use:
    $$ t \in (-\infty,\infty) $$
- Practical rule of thumb from the lecture:
  - use Laplace transforms for initial-value problems starting at $t=0$
  - use Fourier transforms when the independent variable can be negative or ranges over all real values

---

### 15. Main Takeaways
- Convolution is defined by:
  $$ (f*g)(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(\tau)g(t-\tau)\,d\tau $$
- It measures overlap between one function and a shifted, reflected version of another.
- For simple box functions, convolution produces a triangle.
- The Fourier transform of a convolution satisfies:
  $$ \mathcal{F}\{f*g\}=F(\omega)G(\omega) $$
- This theorem is one of the most useful tools in transform methods.
