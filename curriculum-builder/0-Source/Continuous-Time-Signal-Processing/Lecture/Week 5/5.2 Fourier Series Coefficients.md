## Lecture Outline (Fourier Series Coefficients and Complex-Exponential Form)

### 1. Review of Fourier Series for a Periodic Signal
- A periodic signal $x(t)$ can be represented as a sum of sinusoids:
  $$
  x(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[b_k\cos(k\omega_0 t)+a_k\sin(k\omega_0 t)\right]
  $$
- Here:
  - $\omega_0$ is the fundamental frequency
  - $a_k$ and $b_k$ are the Fourier coefficients
- Main idea:
  - any periodic signal can be reconstructed by adding sine and cosine terms
  - the frequencies increase harmonically as $k=1,2,3,\dots$

---

### 2. Meaning of the Fourier Coefficients
- The coefficients determine **how much** of each sine and cosine is needed.
- The signal is built from:
  - the fundamental sinusoid
  - higher harmonics
  - the correct weighting for each harmonic
- Without the coefficients, the sines and cosines alone are not enough to reconstruct $x(t)$.

---

### 3. Complex-Exponential Fourier Series
- The lecture rewrites the Fourier series in a more compact complex-exponential form:
  $$
  x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} A_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}
  $$
- Here:
  - $A_k$ is the complex Fourier-series coefficient
  - this form contains the same information as the sine/cosine form
- Interpretation:
  - the complex-exponential form is a more compact representation
  - each complex coefficient contains amplitude and phase information

---

### 4. Formula for the Complex Fourier Coefficients
- The coefficients are computed over one complete period:
  $$
  A_k=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\text{one period}} x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
  $$
- This tells us exactly how much of the harmonic
  $$ e^{jk\omega_0 t} $$
  is present in the signal.

---

### 5. DC Component / Average Value
- For $k=0$, the coefficient becomes:
  $$
  A_0=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\text{one period}} x(t)\,dt
  $$
- So $A_0$ is the **average value** or **DC component** of the signal.

---

### 6. Example 1: Fourier Series of a Sine Wave
- Let:
  $$
  x(t)=\sin(\omega_0 t)
  $$
- Using Euler’s identity:
  $$
  \sin(\theta)=\frac{e^{j\theta}-e^{-j\theta}}{2j}
  $$
- Therefore:
  $$
  \sin(\omega_0 t)=\frac{1}{2j}e^{j\omega_0 t}-\frac{1}{2j}e^{-j\omega_0 t}
  $$
- Comparing with
  $$
  x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} A_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}
  $$
  gives:
  $$
  A_1=\frac{1}{2j}, \qquad A_{-1}=-\frac{1}{2j}
  $$
  and
  $$
  A_k=0 \quad \text{for all other } k
  $$

---

### 7. Example 2: Periodic Square Wave / PWM Signal
- Consider a periodic square wave with period $T$:
  - amplitude $1$ from $-T_1$ to $T_1$
  - zero elsewhere within one period
- The lecture uses:
  $$
  T_1=\frac{T}{4}
  $$
- Fundamental frequency:
  $$
  \omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T}
  $$
- Also:
  $$
  \omega_0 T = 2\pi
  $$

---

### 8. Compute the DC Term for the Square Wave
- The average value is:
  $$
  A_0=\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T_1}^{T_1} 1\,dt
  $$
- So:
  $$
  A_0=\frac{2T_1}{T}
  $$
- Since $T_1=T/4$:
  $$
  A_0=\frac{1}{2}
  $$

---

### 9. Compute the Other Fourier Coefficients
- For $k\neq 0$:
  $$
  A_k=\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T_1}^{T_1} e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
  $$
- Evaluating the integral gives:
  $$
  A_k=\frac{\sin(k\omega_0 T_1)}{k\pi}
  $$
- Since:
  $$
  T_1=\frac{T}{4}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T}
  $$
  we get:
  $$
  \omega_0 T_1=\frac{\pi}{2}
  $$
- Therefore:
  $$
  A_k=\frac{\sin\left(k\pi/2\right)}{k\pi}, \qquad k\neq 0
  $$

---

### 10. Specific Coefficients for the Square Wave
- From the formula:
  $$
  A_1=\frac{\sin(\pi/2)}{\pi}=\frac{1}{\pi}
  $$
- Similarly:
  $$
  A_{-1}=\frac{1}{\pi}
  $$
- For $k=2$:
  $$
  A_2=\frac{\sin(\pi)}{2\pi}=0
  $$
- For $k=3$:
  $$
  A_3=\frac{\sin(3\pi/2)}{3\pi}=-\frac{1}{3\pi}
  $$
- For $k=-3$:
  $$
  A_{-3}=-\frac{1}{3\pi}
  $$
- For $k=5$:
  $$
  A_5=\frac{1}{5\pi}
  $$
- And similarly:
  $$
  A_{-5}=\frac{1}{5\pi}
  $$

---

### 11. Harmonic Pattern for the Square Wave
- The coefficient pattern is:
  - $A_0=\frac{1}{2}$
  - odd harmonics are nonzero
  - even harmonics are zero
- Example values:
  $$
  A_{\pm 1}=\frac{1}{\pi}, \qquad
  A_{\pm 2}=0, \qquad
  A_{\pm 3}=-\frac{1}{3\pi}, \qquad
  A_{\pm 4}=0, \qquad
  A_{\pm 5}=\frac{1}{5\pi}
  $$
- In this example, the coefficients turn out to be real-valued.

---

### 12. Frequency-Domain View
- The Fourier coefficients can be plotted versus harmonic index $k$.
- This gives the **spectrum** of the signal.
- For the square wave:
  - a nonzero DC term appears at $k=0$
  - nonzero coefficients appear only at odd harmonics
  - the magnitudes decrease as harmonic number increases

---

### 13. Reconstruction of the Square Wave
- The lecture emphasizes that the periodic square wave can be rebuilt by adding:
  - the DC term
  - the harmonics
  - the correct coefficients
- If only finitely many terms are added, the result is an approximation.
- As more and more terms are included, the sum gets closer to the original periodic waveform.

---

### 14. Basis-Function Interpretation
- Sines and cosines act like **basis functions**.
- The lecture compares this to vectors in two-dimensional space:
  $$
  \vec{A}=A_x \hat{i}+A_y \hat{j}
  $$
- Analogy:
  - vector $\vec{A}$ corresponds to the signal $x(t)$
  - $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ correspond to basis directions
  - sines and cosines correspond to basis functions
  - Fourier coefficients correspond to coordinates / weights
- Difference:
  - a 2D vector uses finitely many basis vectors
  - a Fourier series uses infinitely many sinusoidal basis functions

---

### 15. Main Takeaways
- A periodic signal can be represented as:
  $$
  x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} A_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}
  $$
- The coefficients are found from:
  $$
  A_k=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\text{one period}} x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
  $$
- The DC term is:
  $$
  A_0=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\text{one period}} x(t)\,dt
  $$
- For the square wave example:
  $$
  A_0=\frac{1}{2}, \qquad
  A_k=\frac{\sin(k\pi/2)}{k\pi}, \quad k\neq 0
  $$
- Fourier series gives both:
  - a time-domain reconstruction using sinusoids
  - a frequency-domain description using coefficients
