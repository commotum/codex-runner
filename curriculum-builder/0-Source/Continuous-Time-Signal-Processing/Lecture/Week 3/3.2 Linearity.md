## Lecture Outline (Linearity and Motivation for Time Invariance)

### 1. Why Study Linearity and Time Invariance?
- Many real-world systems are **not exactly linear**.
- Even so, nonlinear systems can often be approximated as linear **near an operating point**.
- Idea of local linearization:
  - near a chosen operating point, a nonlinear curve can be approximated by its tangent line
- This makes linear-system tools very useful in practice.

---

### 2. Why Time Invariance Matters
- Real systems are often not perfectly time invariant either.
- System parameters may slowly change over time.
- Example idea:
  - a component value such as capacitance may drift over a long time
  - but over a short enough interval, the system can still be treated as approximately time invariant
- This motivates studying time invariance even when real systems are only approximately time invariant.

---

### 3. System Viewpoint
- A system takes an input signal and produces an output signal:
  $$ x(t) \longrightarrow \mathcal{T} \longrightarrow y(t) $$
- Here:
  - $x(t)$ is the input
  - $y(t)$ is the output
  - $\mathcal{T}$ denotes the system

---

### 4. Superposition Principle (Additivity)
- Suppose:
  $$ x_1(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{T}} y_1(t) $$
  $$ x_2(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{T}} y_2(t) $$
- Then the system satisfies **superposition** if:
  $$ x_1(t)+x_2(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{T}} y_1(t)+y_2(t) $$
- In operator form:
  $$ \mathcal{T}\{x_1(t)+x_2(t)\} = \mathcal{T}\{x_1(t)\} + \mathcal{T}\{x_2(t)\} $$

---

### 5. Homogeneity (Scaling)
- A system satisfies **homogeneity** if scaling the input scales the output by the same amount:
  $$ \mathcal{T}\{\alpha x(t)\} = \alpha \mathcal{T}\{x(t)\} $$
- Interpretation:
  - multiply the input by a constant
  - the output must also be multiplied by the same constant

---

### 6. Linearity Condition
- A system is **linear** if it satisfies both:
  - additivity
  - homogeneity
- Combined into one condition:
  $$ \mathcal{T}\{\alpha x_1(t) + \beta x_2(t)\}
     = \alpha \mathcal{T}\{x_1(t)\} + \beta \mathcal{T}\{x_2(t)\} $$
- If either property fails, the system is **not linear**.

---

### 7. Block-Diagram Interpretation of Additivity
- To test additivity, compare:
  1. pass $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ separately through the system, then add the outputs
  2. add the inputs first, then pass the sum through the system
- If both outputs match, then additivity holds:
  $$ \mathcal{T}\{x_1(t)+x_2(t)\} = y_1(t)+y_2(t) $$

---

### 8. Block-Diagram Interpretation of Homogeneity
- To test homogeneity, compare:
  1. pass $x(t)$ through the system, then scale the output by $\alpha$
  2. scale the input by $\alpha$ first, then pass it through the system
- If both outputs match, then homogeneity holds:
  $$ \mathcal{T}\{\alpha x(t)\} = \alpha y(t) $$

---

### 9. Example 1: Derivative System
- System:
  $$ y(t) = K \frac{d}{dt}x(t) $$
- Test linearity using a combined input:
  $$ x(t) = \alpha x_1(t) + \beta x_2(t) $$
- Then:
  $$ y(t) = K \frac{d}{dt}\big(\alpha x_1(t)+\beta x_2(t)\big) $$
- By linearity of differentiation:
  $$ y(t) = \alpha K\frac{dx_1(t)}{dt} + \beta K\frac{dx_2(t)}{dt} $$
- Therefore:
  $$ y(t) = \alpha y_1(t) + \beta y_2(t) $$
- Conclusion:
  - this system is **linear**

---

### 10. Physical Interpretation of Example 1
- The equation
  $$ y(t) = K\frac{d}{dt}x(t) $$
  is analogous to an inductor relation:
  $$ v(t) = L\frac{di(t)}{dt} $$
- So the lecture concludes:
  - an **inductor is a linear element**
- A similar idea can be checked for a capacitor as well.

---

### 11. Example 2: Constant Offset System
- System:
  $$ y(t) = x(t) + 5 $$
- This may look algebraically simple, but linearity must be tested using the system definition.

#### Additivity test
- Process inputs separately:
  $$ x_1(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{T}} x_1(t)+5 $$
  $$ x_2(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{T}} x_2(t)+5 $$
- Add the outputs:
  $$ \big(x_1(t)+5\big) + \big(x_2(t)+5\big) = x_1(t)+x_2(t)+10 $$
- Now add the inputs first:
  $$ x_1(t)+x_2(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{T}} x_1(t)+x_2(t)+5 $$
- Since:
  $$ x_1(t)+x_2(t)+10 \ne x_1(t)+x_2(t)+5 $$
- Additivity fails.

#### Conclusion
- The system is **not linear**.

---

### 12. Example 3: Squaring System
- System:
  $$ y(t) = x^2(t) $$
- Test additivity:
  $$ x_1(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{T}} x_1^2(t) $$
  $$ x_2(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{T}} x_2^2(t) $$
- Add the separate outputs:
  $$ x_1^2(t) + x_2^2(t) $$
- Now add the inputs first:
  $$ x_1(t)+x_2(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{T}} \big(x_1(t)+x_2(t)\big)^2 $$
- Expand:
  $$ \big(x_1(t)+x_2(t)\big)^2 = x_1^2(t) + 2x_1(t)x_2(t) + x_2^2(t) $$
- Because of the extra term:
  $$ 2x_1(t)x_2(t) $$
  the outputs are not equal.

#### Conclusion
- The system is **not linear**.

---

### 13. Key Observations
- A system is linear only if it satisfies:
  $$ \mathcal{T}\{\alpha x_1(t)+\beta x_2(t)\}
     = \alpha y_1(t)+\beta y_2(t) $$
- Systems can be described by:
  - differential equations
  - algebraic equations
- The form of the equation alone does **not** guarantee linearity.

---

### 14. Main Takeaways
- Real systems are often approximated as linear near an operating point.
- Real systems may also be treated as approximately time invariant over short time intervals.
- The two ingredients of linearity are:
  - additivity
  - homogeneity
- Example conclusions from the lecture:
  - $$ y(t)=K\frac{d}{dt}x(t) $$ is **linear**
  - $$ y(t)=x(t)+5 $$ is **not linear**
  - $$ y(t)=x^2(t) $$ is **not linear**
- This lecture mainly develops **linearity** and motivates **time invariance**, with the formal time-invariance discussion deferred to the next lecture.
