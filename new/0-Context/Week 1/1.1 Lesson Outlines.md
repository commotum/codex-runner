# 1.1 Lesson Outlines

Note
- These outlines aim to capture the full progression of each lesson.
- They should not be read as minimizing section count or padding to a quota.
- In general, lessons in this system should usually land between 4 and 7 TOC sections, with a normal minimum of 3 and maximum of 8.

## 1. Recognizing Periodic Continuous-Time Signals

Outcome: determine whether a continuous-time signal is periodic and find its fundamental period.

Core move: apply the periodicity condition to identify a repeating interval and decide whether it is fundamental.

Optional introduction
- Briefly define periodicity with `x(t) = x(t + T)` for `T > 0`.
- State that the fundamental period is the smallest positive repeating interval.

### Section 1: Recognizing Direct Repetition
- What changes: start with the cleanest case where the repetition interval is stated directly.
- Needs to cover:
  - the definition of a periodic continuous-time signal,
  - the meaning of `T > 0`,
  - a simple pulse-train-style example with an obvious period.

### Section 2: Recognizing Repetition Over Integer Multiples
- What changes: keep the same move, but now make the learner track repetition over `nT` rather than a single shift by `T`.
- Needs to cover:
  - the form `x(t) = x(t + nT)` for `n in Z`,
  - why integer multiples of a period also repeat the signal,
  - examples where several positive periods are visible.

### Section 3: Distinguishing the Fundamental Period
- What changes: keep the same move, but now distinguish the fundamental period from larger valid periods.
- Needs to cover:
  - why `2T`, `3T`, and other multiples are not the fundamental period,
  - the requirement that the fundamental period be the smallest positive period,
  - examples where more than one repeating interval is available.

### Section 4: Finding the Fundamental Period from a Repeating Description
- What changes: the repetition is no longer handed to the learner in the exact form `x(t) = x(t + T)`.
- Needs to cover:
  - reading the repeating interval from a pulse train, graph, or verbal description,
  - identifying the smallest repeating interval,
  - rejecting a larger multiple as the fundamental period.

## 2. Reading Parameters of Real-Valued Sinusoids

Outcome: read the amplitude, angular frequency, period, and phase of a sinusoid.

Core move: extract the parameters of a sinusoid from its standard form and connect angular frequency to period.

Optional introduction
- Present the standard form `x(t) = A sin(omega_0 t + phi)`.
- Name the parameters once before moving into examples.

### Section 1: Reading Amplitude and Angular Frequency
- What changes: start with the cleanest standard-form sinusoid, with no extra interpretation beyond reading parameters.
- Needs to cover:
  - amplitude `A`,
  - angular frequency `omega_0`,
  - the role of each parameter in the waveform,
  - simple standard-form examples.

### Section 2: Connecting Angular Frequency and Period
- What changes: keep the same signal form, but now convert between `omega_0` and `T`.
- Needs to cover:
  - the relationship `omega_0 = 2pi / T`,
  - finding period from angular frequency,
  - finding angular frequency from period,
  - keeping units and notation consistent.

### Section 3: Reading Phase from Standard Form
- What changes: keep the same move, but now the learner must read `phi` as a parameter rather than only reading amplitude and frequency.
- Needs to cover:
  - locating the phase term in `A sin(omega_0 t + phi)`,
  - identifying positive and negative phase values,
  - examples with nonzero phase in standard form.

### Section 4: Interpreting Phase as a Shift
- What changes: keep the same move, but now the learner must interpret what the phase does to the waveform.
- Needs to cover:
  - how phase affects the starting point at `t = 0`,
  - how phase corresponds to a time shift,
  - how phase can connect sine and cosine forms,
  - controlled examples that compare two otherwise identical sinusoids with different phase.

Support note
- Physical examples of sinusoidal behavior should appear as short motivation or illustration inside the introduction or between sections, not as their own lesson section.

## 3. Identifying Harmonics from Fundamental Frequency

Outcome: determine whether a sinusoid is a harmonic of a given fundamental frequency.

Core move: compare a sinusoid's frequency to a fundamental frequency and identify the harmonic number.

Optional introduction
- Define harmonics with `omega_k = k omega_0`.
- State that the harmonic number is the integer multiple relating the two frequencies.

### Section 1: Identifying Simple Harmonics
- What changes: start with the cleanest case where the harmonic number is easy to see from the frequency.
- Needs to cover:
  - the meaning of fundamental frequency,
  - the definition `omega_k = k omega_0`,
  - first, second, and third harmonics.

### Section 2: Reading Harmonic Number from Direct Multiples
- What changes: keep the same move, but now apply the definition to frequencies written directly as multiples of `omega_0`.
- Needs to cover:
  - examples where the frequency is written directly as an integer multiple of `omega_0`,
  - matching a sinusoid to its harmonic number,
  - distinguishing the fundamental from higher harmonics.

### Section 3: Finding Harmonic Number from a Given Frequency
- What changes: keep the same move, but now the learner must infer the harmonic number by comparing two explicit frequencies.
- Needs to cover:
  - comparing a given sinusoid's frequency to the fundamental,
  - identifying `k` in `omega_k = k omega_0`,
  - examples like `2pi`, `4pi`, and `6pi` relative to a base frequency.

### Section 4: Recognizing Harmonic Structure in a Periodic Signal
- What changes: move from a single sinusoid to a small collection of harmonically related components.
- Needs to cover:
  - recognizing that periodic signals can be built from harmonically related sinusoids,
  - identifying which components belong to the same harmonic family,
  - a short bridge to why harmonics matter later.

## 4. Determining When Sums Stay Periodic

Outcome: determine whether a sum of periodic signals is periodic from their period ratio.

Core move: test whether two periods have a common multiple by checking whether their ratio is rational.

Optional introduction
- Set up a linear combination such as `z(t) = a x(t) + b y(t)`.
- State that this lesson is about sums and linear combinations of periodic signals.

### Section 1: Finding a Common Period in the Clean Case
- What changes: start with periods that have an obvious common multiple.
- Needs to cover:
  - a simple linear combination such as `z(t) = a x(t) + b y(t)`,
  - the condition `T = m T_1 = n T_2`,
  - finding a shared period from simple values,
  - seeing how the sum inherits periodicity when both signals line up again.

### Section 2: Expressing the Common-Period Condition
- What changes: keep the same move, but now express the shared-period idea algebraically rather than just spotting it from examples.
- Needs to cover:
  - the meaning of `T = m T_1 = n T_2`,
  - how integers `m` and `n` encode when the signals realign,
  - examples where the common-period condition is set up explicitly.

### Section 3: Using the Rational-Ratio Test for Sums
- What changes: keep the same move, but now decide periodicity from the relationship between the two periods.
- Needs to cover:
  - the equivalent condition `T_1 / T_2 = n / m`,
  - why a rational ratio implies a common period,
  - examples where the learner decides whether the sum is periodic from the ratio alone.

### Section 4: Distinguishing Sums from Other Operations
- What changes: keep the periodicity question, but clarify that this rule is about sums and linear combinations rather than products.
- Needs to cover:
  - addition as a linear operation,
  - why this lesson's rule belongs to sums and linear combinations,
  - a short setup for the next lesson on multiplication.

## 5. Applying Product-to-Sum to Signal Products

Outcome: rewrite a product of sinusoids to identify the frequencies it contains.

Core move: use the product-to-sum identity to convert a product into sinusoidal components and read off the resulting frequencies.

Optional introduction
- State that multiplication is a nonlinear operation.
- Introduce the product-to-sum identity before the first example.

### Section 1: Rewriting a Product with Different Frequencies
- What changes: start with the setup for a product of two sinusoids and the identity that rewrites it.
- Needs to cover:
  - multiplication as a nonlinear operation,
  - the form `cos(omega_1 t) cos(omega_2 t)`,
  - why a new identity is needed for products,
  - the product-to-sum identity,
  - a first clean example with different frequencies.

### Section 2: Reading Sum and Difference Frequencies
- What changes: keep the same move, but now focus on identifying the frequencies that appear after rewriting.
- Needs to cover:
  - identifying the sum and difference frequencies that appear after rewriting.

### Section 3: Handling the Equal-Frequency Case
- What changes: keep the same move, but now both sinusoids have the same frequency.
- Needs to cover:
  - substituting `omega_1 = omega_2 = omega`,
  - simplifying the product into a DC term plus a cosine at `2 omega`,
  - recognizing the second harmonic in the result.

### Section 4: Interpreting Harmonic Generation from Nonlinear Operations
- What changes: move from algebraic rewriting to interpreting what the new components mean.
- Needs to cover:
  - why multiplication can create frequencies not visible in the original factors,
  - how a nonlinear operation can generate higher-order harmonics,
  - the specific role of the DC term and the second harmonic in the equal-frequency example.
