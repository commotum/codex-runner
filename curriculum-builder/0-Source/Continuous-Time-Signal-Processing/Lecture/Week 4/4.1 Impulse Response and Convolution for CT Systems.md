## Lecture Outline (Continuous-Time Impulse Response and Convolution)

### 1. Unit Impulse Response for Continuous-Time LTI Systems
- The simplest continuous-time input is the unit impulse:
  $$ \delta(t) $$
- If a continuous-time LTI system is excited by $\delta(t)$, the output is the **impulse response**:
  $$ h(t) $$
- The impulse response is extremely important because it **completely characterizes** the system.

---

### 2. Why the Impulse Response Matters
- For an LTI system, once $h(t)$ is known, the output for **any** input $x(t)$ can be found.
- The key idea is:
  - build an arbitrary input out of shifted/scaled impulses
  - use linearity and time invariance
  - add up all of the corresponding shifted/scaled outputs

---

### 3. Review: Continuous-Time Sampling / Sifting Property
- A signal can be represented using impulses as:
  $$ x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)\,\delta(t-\tau)\,d\tau $$
- Interpretation:
  - the signal is viewed as an infinite sum of impulse samples
  - each impulse at time $\tau$ is weighted by the value $x(\tau)$
- This is the continuous-time version of the **sampling** or **sifting** idea.

---

### 4. Shifted and Scaled Impulses Through an LTI System
- If:
  $$ \delta(t) \xrightarrow{\text{LTI}} h(t) $$
- Then by time invariance:
  $$ \delta(t-\tau) \xrightarrow{\text{LTI}} h(t-\tau) $$
- By linearity, scaling the input scales the output:
  $$ x(\tau)\delta(t-\tau) \xrightarrow{\text{LTI}} x(\tau)h(t-\tau) $$

---

### 5. Deriving the Output for a General Input
- Since:
  $$ x(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)\delta(t-\tau)\,d\tau $$
- The corresponding output is:
  $$ y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau $$
- This is the **continuous-time convolution integral**.

---

### 6. Convolution Integral
- Main result:
  $$ y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)\,h(t-\tau)\,d\tau $$
- Shorthand notation:
  $$ y(t)=x(t)*h(t) $$
- Here $*$ denotes **convolution**, not ordinary multiplication.

---

### 7. Main Interpretation of Convolution
- To compute $y(t)$:
  1. rewrite the input as $x(\tau)$
  2. time-reverse the impulse response to get $h(-\tau)$
  3. shift it to get $h(t-\tau)$
  4. multiply $x(\tau)$ and $h(t-\tau)$
  5. integrate over all $\tau$
- The output depends on the **overlap** between $x(\tau)$ and the shifted/reversed $h(t-\tau)$.

---

### 8. Example 1: Exponential Input and Exponential Impulse Response
- Given:
  $$ x(t)=e^{-at}u(t) $$
  $$ h(t)=e^{-bt}u(t) $$
- The output is:
  $$ y(t)=x(t)*h(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau $$

---

### 9. Overlap Analysis for Example 1
- For $t<0$, there is no overlap between $x(\tau)$ and $h(t-\tau)$, so:
  $$ y(t)=0 $$
- For $t\ge 0$, the overlap runs from $\tau=0$ to $\tau=t$, so:
  $$ y(t)=\int_{0}^{t} e^{-a\tau}e^{-b(t-\tau)}\,d\tau $$

---

### 10. Final Result for Example 1
#### Case 1: $a=b$
- Then:
  $$ y(t)=te^{-bt}u(t) $$

#### Case 2: $a\ne b$
- Then:
  $$ y(t)=\frac{e^{-at}-e^{-bt}}{b-a}\,u(t) $$

---

### 11. Harder Example: Rectangular Input and Finite-Duration Impulse Response
- Input:
  $$
  x(t)=
  \begin{cases}
  2, & 0\le t \le 3 \\
  0, & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  $$
- Impulse response:
  $$
  h(t)=
  \begin{cases}
  -2t+4, & 0\le t \le 2 \\
  0, & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  $$

---

### 12. Piecewise Convolution Result for the Harder Example
- By sliding $h(t-\tau)$ across $x(\tau)$ and analyzing the overlap region, the output is:
  $$
  y(t)=
  \begin{cases}
  0, & t<0 \\
  -2t^2+8t, & 0\le t \le 2 \\
  8, & 2\le t \le 3 \\
  2t^2-20t+50, & 3\le t \le 5 \\
  0, & t>5
  \end{cases}
  $$
- The lecture emphasizes that this is much more tedious because:
  - the overlap changes in different time ranges
  - the functional form must be re-derived in each interval

---

### 13. Geometric View of Convolution
- In more difficult problems, convolution can often be understood geometrically:
  - identify the overlap interval
  - find the function over that overlap
  - integrate the shaded area
- Sometimes this requires line equations, triangle areas, or similar-triangle arguments.

---

### 14. Commutative Property of Convolution
- Convolution is **commutative**:
  $$ x(t)*h(t)=h(t)*x(t) $$
- This means the order of the two signals can be swapped without changing the result.

---

### 15. Why Commutativity Is Useful
- In hard problems, it may be easier to reverse and shift one signal instead of the other.
- For the rectangular-pulse example, the lecture shows that flipping/shifting the rectangular pulse is easier than flipping/shifting the sloped impulse response.
- Same answer, easier work:
  $$ x(t)*h(t)=h(t)*x(t) $$

---

### 16. Big Picture About Convolution
- Convolution gives the output of an LTI system from:
  - the input $x(t)$
  - the impulse response $h(t)$
- This is why the impulse response is such a powerful system description.

---

### 17. Final Takeaways
- Continuous-time convolution:
  $$ y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau $$
- Equivalent shorthand:
  $$ y(t)=x(t)*h(t) $$
- The impulse response completely characterizes an LTI system.
- Convolution by hand is often tedious and messy.
- Later, frequency-domain methods (Fourier series / Fourier transforms) provide much easier ways to handle these problems.

---

### 18. Forward Connection
- The lecture ends by noting that direct convolution is a necessary foundation,
