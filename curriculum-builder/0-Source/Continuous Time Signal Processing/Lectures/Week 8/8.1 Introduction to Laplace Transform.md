## Lecture Outline (From Fourier Transform to Laplace Transform)

### 1. Review of the Fourier Transform
- The Fourier transform of a time-domain signal $x(t)$ is:
  $$ X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt $$
- This maps a signal from the **time domain** to the **frequency domain** for analysis.

---

### 2. Review of Frequency Response for an LTI System
- For an LTI system with impulse response $h(t)$, the frequency response is:
  $$ H(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt $$
- This is the Fourier transform of the impulse response.
- If the input is a sinusoid / complex exponential:
  $$ x(t) = e^{j\omega t} $$
- Then the output is:
  $$ y(t) = H(j\omega)e^{j\omega t} $$
- Interpretation:
  - the output is also sinusoidal
  - the output has the **same frequency** $\omega$
  - the amplitude and phase are modified by $H(j\omega)$

---

### 3. Example: RC Circuit Frequency Response
- For the RC low-pass circuit discussed earlier, the frequency response is:
  $$ H(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC} $$
- This result was derived assuming a sinusoidal input.

---

### 4. Motivation for Laplace Transform
- Suppose the RC circuit input is not sinusoidal, but instead a step input:
  $$ x(t) = V_{in}u(t) $$
- If we try to apply the Fourier transform directly:
  $$ X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} V_{in}u(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
     = V_{in}\int_0^{\infty} e^{-j\omega t}\,dt $$
- Under the ordinary integral definition used in the lecture, this does not converge.
- So Fourier transform is not always sufficient for analyzing such signals.

---

### 5. Definition of the Laplace Transform
- To handle a wider class of signals, define the Laplace transform:
  $$ X(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-st}\,dt $$
- Similarly, for an impulse response:
  $$ H(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(t)e^{-st}\,dt $$

---

### 6. Complex Variable $s$
- In Laplace transform:
  $$ s = \sigma + j\omega $$
- Here:
  - $\sigma$ is the real part
  - $j\omega$ is the imaginary part
- Because of the real part $\sigma$, Laplace transform introduces an exponential weighting factor that can improve convergence.

---

### 7. Laplace Transform as a Generalization of Fourier Transform
- If:
  $$ \sigma = 0 $$
- Then:
  $$ s = j\omega $$
- So the Laplace transform reduces to the Fourier transform:
  $$ X(s) \to X(j\omega) $$
- Therefore, Laplace transform is a **generalization** of the Fourier transform.

---

### 8. Why the Real Part Helps
- In the Laplace kernel:
  $$ e^{-st} = e^{-(\sigma + j\omega)t} = e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t} $$
- The factor:
  $$ e^{-\sigma t} $$
  adds exponential decay when $\sigma > 0$.
- This can make an otherwise divergent integral converge.

---

### 9. Example Signal
- Consider:
  $$ x(t) = e^{-at}u(t), \qquad a>0 $$

---

### 10. Fourier Transform of the Example
- Since the signal is one-sided:
  $$ X(j\omega) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-at}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt $$
- Combine exponentials:
  $$ X(j\omega) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-(a+j\omega)t}\,dt $$
- Evaluating gives:
  $$ X(j\omega) = \frac{1}{a+j\omega} $$

---

### 11. Laplace Transform of the Example
- Apply the Laplace transform:
  $$ X(s) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-at}e^{-st}\,dt $$
- Combine exponentials:
  $$ X(s) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-(s+a)t}\,dt $$
- Evaluating:
  $$ X(s) = \frac{1}{s+a} $$

---

### 12. Region of Convergence (ROC)
- The transform
  $$ X(s)=\frac{1}{s+a} $$
  is valid only when the integral converges.
- Convergence requires:
  $$ \Re\{s+a\} > 0 $$
- Equivalently:
  $$ \Re\{s\} > -a $$
- Since $s=\sigma+j\omega$, this means:
  $$ \sigma > -a $$

---

### 13. $s$-Plane Interpretation
- In the $s$-plane:
  - horizontal axis: $\sigma$
  - vertical axis: $j\omega$
- For the example
  $$ X(s)=\frac{1}{s+a} $$
  the ROC is the half-plane:
  $$ \Re\{s\} > -a $$
- So the region of convergence lies to the **right** of the vertical line at:
  $$ \sigma = -a $$

---

### 14. Main Takeaways
- Fourier transform:
  $$ X(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt $$
- Laplace transform:
  $$ X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-st}\,dt $$
- Laplace transform generalizes Fourier transform through:
  $$ s=\sigma+j\omega $$
- If $\sigma=0$, Laplace reduces to Fourier.
- The extra real exponential factor in Laplace transform helps convergence.
- Example transform pair:
  $$ e^{-at}u(t) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{s+a}, \qquad \Re\{s\}>-a $$

---

### 15. Why This Matters for Circuits and Systems
- Fourier transform works well for sinusoidal steady-state analysis.
- Laplace transform can handle broader classes of inputs, including signals like steps.
- This makes Laplace transform especially useful for analyzing practical circuit and system responses.
