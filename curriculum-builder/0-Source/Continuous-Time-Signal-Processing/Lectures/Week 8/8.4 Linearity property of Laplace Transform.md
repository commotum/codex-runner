## Lecture Outline (Laplace Transform Property: Linearity)

### 1. Review of the Laplace Transform
- For the causal signals used in this lecture, the Laplace transform is:
  $$ X(s) = \int_{0}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-st}\,dt $$
- This maps a time-domain signal $x(t)$ to its $s$-domain representation $X(s)$.

---

### 2. Linearity Property of the Laplace Transform
- If:
  $$ x_1(t) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; X_1(s) $$
  $$ x_2(t) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; X_2(s) $$
- Then for constants $a_1$ and $a_2$:
  $$ a_1x_1(t) + a_2x_2(t) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; a_1X_1(s) + a_2X_2(s) $$
- This is the Laplace-transform version of:
  - scaling
  - superposition

---

### 3. Proof of Linearity
- Start from the definition:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\{a_1x_1(t)+a_2x_2(t)\}
     = \int_{0}^{\infty} \left(a_1x_1(t)+a_2x_2(t)\right)e^{-st}\,dt $$
- Split the integral:
  $$ = a_1\int_{0}^{\infty} x_1(t)e^{-st}\,dt
     + a_2\int_{0}^{\infty} x_2(t)e^{-st}\,dt $$
- Recognize each term:
  $$ = a_1X_1(s) + a_2X_2(s) $$
- Therefore, the Laplace transform is linear.

---

### 4. Known Transform Pair Used in the Example
- A standard result:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\{e^{-at}u(t)\} = \frac{1}{s+a} $$
- This is used repeatedly in the worked example.

---

### 5. Example Signals
- Given:
  $$ x_1(t) = e^{-t}u(t) $$
  $$ x_2(t) = e^{-t}u(t) - e^{-2t}u(t) $$
- Define:
  $$ x(t) = x_1(t) + x_2(t) $$
- Goal: find the Laplace transform of $x(t)$.

---

### 6. Laplace Transform of $x_1(t)$
- Using the standard pair with $a=1$:
  $$ X_1(s) = \frac{1}{s+1} $$

---

### 7. Laplace Transform of $x_2(t)$
- Apply linearity to the two terms:
  $$ X_2(s) = \mathcal{L}\{e^{-t}u(t)\} - \mathcal{L}\{e^{-2t}u(t)\} $$
- Therefore:
  $$ X_2(s) = \frac{1}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+2} $$
- Combine into one fraction:
  $$ X_2(s) = \frac{(s+2)-(s+1)}{(s+1)(s+2)} $$
  $$ X_2(s) = \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)} $$

---

### 8. Use Linearity to Find $X(s)$
- Since:
  $$ x(t) = x_1(t) + x_2(t) $$
- By linearity:
  $$ X(s) = X_1(s) + X_2(s) $$
- Substitute the two results:
  $$ X(s) = \frac{1}{s+1} + \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)} $$
- Put over a common denominator:
  $$ X(s) = \frac{s+2}{(s+1)(s+2)} + \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)} $$
- Final result:
  $$ X(s) = \frac{s+3}{(s+1)(s+2)} $$

---

### 9. Main Problem-Solving Strategy
- When a signal is written as a sum of simpler signals:
  1. Find the Laplace transform of each part separately
  2. Use linearity to combine the results
- This is often much easier than transforming the full expression at once.

---

### 10. Main Takeaways
- The Laplace transform is linear:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\{a_1x_1(t)+a_2x_2(t)\} = a_1X_1(s)+a_2X_2(s) $$
- A very useful transform pair is:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\{e^{-at}u(t)\} = \frac{1}{s+a} $$
- For the example in the lecture:
  $$ x(t)=x_1(t)+x_2(t) $$
  the final Laplace transform is:
  $$ X(s)=\frac{s+3}{(s+1)(s+2)} $$
