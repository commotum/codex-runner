## Lecture Outline (Laplace Transform: Time-Derivative Property)

### 1. Main Goal
- Introduce the **time-derivative property** of the Laplace transform.
- Key idea:
  - differentiation in the time domain becomes an **algebraic operation** in the $s$-domain
- This is what makes Laplace transforms so useful for solving differential equations.

---

### 2. First-Derivative Property
- If:
  $$ x(t) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; X(s) $$
- Then:
  $$ \frac{d}{dt}x(t) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; sX(s) - x(0^-) $$
- Interpretation:
  - taking one derivative in time corresponds to multiplying by $s$
  - but you must subtract the initial condition $x(0^-)$

---

### 3. Second-Derivative Property
- For the second derivative:
  $$ \frac{d^2}{dt^2}x(t) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; s^2X(s) - sx(0^-) - \dot{x}(0^-) $$
- This extends the first-derivative rule and introduces both:
  - initial position:
    $$ x(0^-) $$
  - initial slope / velocity:
    $$ \dot{x}(0^-) $$

---

### 4. Meaning of Initial Conditions
- The terms $x(0^-)$ and $\dot{x}(0^-)$ represent the condition of the signal **just before** $t=0$.
- Physical interpretation examples from the lecture:
  - a **pre-charged capacitor**, where the capacitor voltage at $t=0$ is not zero
  - a **spring-mass system** pulled away from equilibrium before release, so the initial displacement is not zero

---

### 5. Example Signal
- The lecture solves a piecewise triangular signal:
  - rises linearly from $0$ to $2$ over $0 \le t \le 2$
  - then decreases linearly back to $0$ over $2 \le t \le 3$
  - zero elsewhere
- The goal is to find:
  $$ X(s) $$

---

### 6. First Derivative of the Example Signal
- The first derivative is piecewise constant:
  - slope $1$ over the rising segment
  - slope $-2$ over the falling segment
  - zero elsewhere
- So:
  $$
  \frac{d}{dt}x(t)=
  \begin{cases}
  1, & 0<t<2 \\
  -2, & 2<t<3 \\
  0, & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  $$

---

### 7. Second Derivative of the Example Signal
- Since the first derivative has jumps, the second derivative becomes a sum of impulses.
- The jumps are:
  - from $0$ to $1$ at $t=0$  $\Rightarrow$ impulse of weight $1$
  - from $1$ to $-2$ at $t=2$ $\Rightarrow$ impulse of weight $-3$
  - from $-2$ to $0$ at $t=3$ $\Rightarrow$ impulse of weight $2$
- Therefore:
  $$ \frac{d^2}{dt^2}x(t) = \delta(t) - 3\delta(t-2) + 2\delta(t-3) $$

---

### 8. Laplace Transform of the Right-Hand Side
- Use:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\{\delta(t)\} = 1 $$
- And the time-shifting property:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\{\delta(t-a)\} = e^{-as} $$
- So:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{d^2}{dt^2}x(t)\right\} = 1 - 3e^{-2s} + 2e^{-3s} $$

---

### 9. Laplace Transform of the Left-Hand Side
- Apply the second-derivative property:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{d^2}{dt^2}x(t)\right\} = s^2X(s) - sx(0^-) - \dot{x}(0^-) $$
- For this signal, the lecture assumes:
  $$ x(0^-) = 0, \qquad \dot{x}(0^-) = 0 $$
- Therefore:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{d^2}{dt^2}x(t)\right\} = s^2X(s) $$

---

### 10. Solve for $X(s)$
- Equate both sides:
  $$ s^2X(s) = 1 - 3e^{-2s} + 2e^{-3s} $$
- So:
  $$ X(s) = \frac{1}{s^2} - \frac{3e^{-2s}}{s^2} + \frac{2e^{-3s}}{s^2} $$

---

### 11. Why This Method Is Useful
- In the previous lecture, the same signal was solved using only time-shifting and step functions.
- That method worked, but it took more algebra.
- Here, by using derivatives:
  - the original piecewise signal becomes a much simpler impulse expression
  - the Laplace transform becomes easier to compute
- This shows the real power of the derivative property.

---

### 12. Main Takeaways
- First derivative:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}x(t)\right\} = sX(s) - x(0^-) $$
- Second derivative:
  $$ \mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{d^2}{dt^2}x(t)\right\} = s^2X(s) - sx(0^-) - \dot{x}(0^-) $$
- For the worked example:
  $$ \frac{d^2}{dt^2}x(t) = \delta(t) - 3\delta(t-2) + 2\delta(t-3) $$
- Final Laplace transform:
  $$ X(s) = \frac{1}{s^2} - \frac{3e^{-2s}}{s^2} + \frac{2e^{-3s}}{s^2} $$

---

### 13. Big Picture
- The derivative property converts a differential-equation problem into an algebra problem in $s$.
- This is one of the most important reasons Laplace transforms are so useful in circuits, systems, and differential equations.
